The practice

How institutional systems model the world — and what they cannot see

Contemporary institutions — councils, insurers, platform companies, planning bodies — generate large quantities of data about the populations and places they govern. They measure participation, satisfaction, risk, wellbeing, land use, demographic change. Their models are increasingly sophisticated.

Yet the gaps persist. Communities resist development that the data said they wanted. Households are locked out of systems designed to serve them. Platforms produce divergent and sometimes catastrophic outcomes for users the aggregate models said were fine. Cities invest in belonging and see attachment decline.

The pattern is consistent: the systems are observing the world at the wrong level. They measure outputs and proxies. They aggregate across populations in ways that erase the variance that matters. They are calibrated to a model of the household, the community, the user, the place — and the model is wrong in specific, structural ways.

Superfutures works below the level of indicators and above the level of anecdote. The primary methodology is assemblage analysis — examining how institutional systems are composed, what they make visible and what they structurally cannot see, and how they behave when the social world they are acting on does not conform to their assumptions.

Relational Systems Simulation (RSS) is one instrument of this methodology: a constrained large language model approach to modelling how culturally complex actors navigate institutional systems, surfacing where the institutional model of those actors fails and with what consequences. The Auckland Observatory, Animal Spirits, and Controlled Hallucination are further instruments operating at different scales and in different domains — each making visible something the dominant observational systems in its field cannot register.

The work produces findings that are simultaneously theoretically rigorous, empirically grounded, and practically actionable.

Live instruments

What does collective economic affect look like when you instrument it rather than aggregate it?

Animal Spirits

A live observatory tracking collective economic affect across three regions. Drawing on Keynes's original account of animal spirits — the role of confidence, narrative, and collective mood in economic behaviour — and theorised through DeLanda, Collins, and Bakhtin, Animal Spirits instruments the gap between aggregate market data and the affective conditions that actually drive economic behaviour. Developed with Maurice Benayoun, Hong Kong.

What does the boundary between perception and confabulation look like from inside the generative process?

Controlled Hallucination

An experimental artefact exploring the generative mechanism by which language models produce plausible continuations — what Karl Friston calls the brain's fundamental operation, extended here to machine cognition. Where confabulation becomes visible, the conditions for positional integrity — or its absence — become auditable.

What are cities structurally unable to see about the places they govern?

Affective Observatory

An observational infrastructure for the dimensions of urban place value that planning, development, and governance systems structurally cannot register: attachment, recognition, ecological identity, continuity, and the processes through which people become part of — or are excluded from — the places they live. Developed as an RSS application at urban scale. Comprises an observatory layer and a participatory capture interface.

Research outputs

What happens when financial systems encounter households they were not designed to see?

Beyond the Score

An RSS pilot examining how banking, insurance, and verification systems interact with four culturally distinct New Zealand households — a Samoan-NZ extended family, a Pākehā three-generation household, a Chinese-NZ migrant family, and a rural Māori whānau — navigating open banking, insurance withdrawal, algorithmic risk scoring, and climate-linked displacement pressures. The expected finding was that Māori and Pacific households would be misread. What was less expected was that every household was misread — just differently. There is no unmarked baseline that the algorithm sees clearly.

What does the RSS pilot reveal about the assumptions embedded in financial and welfare systems?

The Household That Doesn't Exist

Algorithmic decision systems are built around a model of the household that does not exist. This essay reports on the Beyond the Score pilot and examines what it reveals about the structural assumptions embedded in credit scoring, insurance assessment, and welfare eligibility — and about the forms of social life, resilience, and relational support that institutional systems fail to register. Combines a critical essay with AI-generated stills, using generative media not to illustrate households but to render the limits of what institutional systems are able to see.

What does a mechanism-level account of digitally mediated communication reveal that platform governance currently misses?

What Language Does

A theoretical paper developing a mechanism-level account of why the same digital platform produces divergent outcomes across users. The mechanism is communication itself — addressed expression operating through four conditions: elaboration, ratification, narrative continuity, and positional integrity. Where these conditions are present, self-organisation proceeds. Where they are absent or distorted, the same environment produces harm. The framework has direct implications for AI governance: sycophancy is not a design quirk but the systematic destruction of positional integrity. Restriction-oriented policy calibrated to the modal user systematically removes the communicative infrastructure most necessary for the population it most needs to protect.

What are cities structurally unable to see, and what becomes possible when they do?

Auckland Observatory

An observational infrastructure and research programme for the dimensions of urban place value that planning, development, and governance systems structurally cannot register. Drawing on Guattari's Three Ecologies, Scott's account of legibility and administrative simplification, and RSS as methodological foundation, AO develops participatory and ambient methods for observing attachment, recognition, ecological identity, and the processes through which subjectivation occurs in specific urban places. Pilot sites: Te Auaunga, Avondale, Aotea Square.

About

Leon Tan

PhD · Researcher & Practitioner
Design & Contemporary Arts, Unitec
Tāmaki Makaurau Auckland

Leon Tan is a researcher and practitioner whose work examines how institutional and computational systems model the social world — and what they structurally cannot see.

His research spans urban governance, financial systems, AI infrastructure, and the communicative conditions of social life. He develops and applies assemblage-informed research methods, including Relational Systems Simulation and the Affective Observatory, to produce findings that are simultaneously theoretically rigorous, empirically grounded, and practically actionable.

His intellectual commitments run through Deleuze and Guattari, Félix Guattari's Three Ecologies, James C. Scott on legibility and governance, Science and Technology Studies, and a sustained engagement with Māori, Pacific, and non-Western epistemological traditions as primary rather than supplementary analytical frameworks.

He works with researchers, designers, policymakers, urban practitioners, and institutional leaders on problems where the gap between how systems model the world and how the world actually lives is consequential.

Enquiries

Superfutures takes a limited number of research and consulting engagements each year, working with institutions and organisations where the gap between how systems model the social world and how that world actually functions is producing consequences that existing frameworks cannot adequately address.

Engagements typically combine research and consulting functions — producing findings that advance the intellectual programme while addressing a specific institutional problem. We do not separate the research from the application.
